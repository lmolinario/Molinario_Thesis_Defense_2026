## Slide 1 — Title

Good morning, and thank you for the opportunity to present my work.

My thesis is entitled **“Evaluating the Robustness of AI-Based Forensic Tools under Adversarial and Anti-Forensic Attacks.”**

The starting point of this research is the increasing use of Artificial Intelligence in digital forensic tools, especially for automatically analysing and prioritising large collections of images.

The objective of my work was **not to develop a new firearm classifier**.

Instead, I wanted to investigate a more operational question: **can AI-assisted forensic triage still be considered dependable when the images it processes are outside the expected distribution, degraded, or deliberately manipulated?**

This distinction is important because, in a forensic context, classification is not simply an academic machine-learning task. The output of an AI system can influence which material an analyst sees first and, potentially, which material receives less attention.

So, I will begin with the operational risk that motivated the entire study.

---

## Slide 2 — The operational risk is silent exclusion

Digital forensic investigations can involve thousands, or even hundreds of thousands, of images.

Artificial Intelligence can therefore be extremely useful to reduce the analyst's workload by identifying potentially relevant material and placing it higher in the review queue.

However, the two possible directions of error do not have the same operational consequence.

A **false positive** generally means that the analyst has to inspect an additional irrelevant image. This increases workload.

A **false negative**, on the other hand, may be more critical. A relevant image can be classified as non-relevant and silently moved away from the analyst's priority queue.

The evidence itself is not physically lost, but its **discovery and prioritisation path can be affected**.

This is why high accuracy under normal, clean conditions is not sufficient to establish that an AI-assisted forensic system is operationally robust.

The central question of this thesis is therefore:

**How does forensic image triage behave under adversarial, anti-forensic and out-of-distribution inputs?**

My contribution is an **auditable robustness assessment**, rather than a new classification algorithm.

To answer this question systematically, I developed the FAIRLab experimental protocol.

---

## Slide 3 — FAIRLab makes robustness auditable

FAIRLab structures the evaluation into six connected stages.

First, I start from a **heterogeneous source pool** of images.

Second, those images are technically validated, manually reviewed and deduplicated.

Third, I freeze the experimental corpus. Once frozen, the population used for the evaluation no longer changes.

Fourth, I generate controlled adversarial and anti-forensic variants.

Fifth, I evaluate both transparent machine-learning models and commercial forensic systems.

And finally, I normalise and interpret the resulting outputs in operational forensic terms.

An important aspect of FAIRLab is that **traceability is not added at the end**. It is maintained throughout the entire pipeline.

Images have deterministic identifiers; datasets and experimental configurations are frozen; manifests link source images to their derived variants; checksums protect controlled artefacts; and the final reported results can be linked back to the corresponding source and experimental condition.

This allows us to ask not only whether a system made an error, but also **which input produced it, under which condition, using which configuration and through which processing path**.

The next slide shows the exact population used in the experiments.

---

## Slide 4 — One population becomes 11,500 traceable files

The frozen corpus contains **1,500 source images**.

Five hundred are classified as **weapon**, five hundred as **non-weapon**, and another five hundred form a separate **out-of-distribution, or OOD, branch**.

The weapon and non-weapon images form the common binary classification task.

For each of these one thousand binary images, I generated ten linked manipulated variants: five adversarial or adversarial-style conditions and five anti-forensic transformations.

This produces **10,000 manipulated files**.

Together with the original 1,500 clean source images, the complete tool-facing bundle therefore contains **11,500 files**.

There are two methodological points that are important here.

First, these 10,000 manipulated images are **not 10,000 independent observations**. Each variant remains explicitly linked to its original source image.

Second, the 500 OOD images are not treated as a third supervised class. Their purpose is different: they allow us to observe how a closed-set system behaves when it receives material that does not properly belong to the binary task.

The same frozen experimental population is then evaluated at two complementary levels.

---

## Slide 5 — Two evidence levels, one experimental basis

The first level consists of three **transparent proxy models**: EfficientNet-B0, ResNet18 and a CLIP-based proxy.

These models provide a controlled environment in which I can inspect predictions, gradients and fold-specific checkpoints.

They are not intended to reverse engineer or reproduce the internal models of commercial forensic products.

Their purpose is to provide an inspectable experimental layer.

The second level consists of **four commercial forensic systems evaluated in six configurations**: Magnet AXIOM with Magnet.AI, Excire Foto 2025 at three distance settings, Cellebrite Inseyets, and Magnet Griffeye with T3K CORE.

In this second case, the systems are black boxes. I can observe the inputs provided to the software and the outputs presented to the analyst, but I do not know their internal architecture, preprocessing, training data or decision logic.

This distinction is also essential for the threat model.

The model-dependent adversarial attacks are generated **directly against EfficientNet-B0**.

When the same generated artefacts are evaluated on ResNet18, CLIP or the commercial tools, I am measuring **empirical transfer**, not a direct attack against those systems.

Color Shift and the five anti-forensic transformations are instead model-agnostic conditions.

Because the commercial systems produce very different types of outputs, the next methodological problem is how to compare them correctly.

---

## Slide 6 — Explicit mappings enable comparison

The commercial products do not expose a common prediction interface.

Depending on the tool, the analyst may see tags, bookmarks, semantic search results or different types of labels.

It would therefore be incorrect to treat all these outputs as if they were equivalent probabilities.

For each version and configuration, I instead defined and froze an explicit **output-normalisation rule**.

The observable output is converted into one of three operational states: **weapon, non-weapon or unknown**.

Once this mapping is explicit, directional errors can be interpreted consistently.

The **False Negative Rate**, or FNR, represents the missed-target risk: a weapon image moves toward the non-weapon state.

The **False Positive Rate**, or FPR, instead represents additional review workload: a non-weapon image is routed toward weapon.

The OOD weapon-assignment rate is kept separate, because OOD images do not have a supervised positive or negative role in the binary experiment.

In the 69,000 normalised commercial decisions produced by the experiment, no unknown state occurred. Nevertheless, the unknown state remains explicitly supported by the protocol.

With these definitions in place, we can now look at the first major robustness result.

---

## Slide 7 — Direct-target collapse did not transfer uniformly

This slide shows why **clean performance and robustness must not be confused**.

EfficientNet-B0 achieves the highest clean accuracy among the proxy models, at **97.8 percent**.

However, under the strongest direct Sigma Zero condition, its accuracy falls to only **1.5 percent**.

This is a decrease of **96.3 percentage points**.

So, a model that performs extremely well under nominal conditions can become extremely vulnerable when an attack is specifically optimised against it.

However, the same adversarial artefacts do **not** produce an equivalent collapse on the other architectures.

For ResNet18, the worst transferred accuracy shown here is **91.8 percent**, under FGSM.

For the CLIP-based proxy, it is **91.4 percent**, again under FGSM.

This distinction is fundamental.

The EfficientNet result demonstrates a **direct-target vulnerability**.

The ResNet18 and CLIP results measure only **empirical transfer from another source model**.

A limited transfer effect does not prove that those models would be robust against attacks constructed directly against them.

Anti-forensic transformations produced smaller aggregate effects on the proxies. The largest observed drop was **3 percentage points**, for ResNet18 under histogram modification.

The same transfer question becomes even more interesting when we move to the proprietary systems.

---

## Slide 8 — Black-box transfer was configuration-dependent

This heatmap extends the analysis to the commercial forensic configurations.

There is no need to read every cell. The important information is the overall pattern.

The effects are **highly heterogeneous**.

Exactly the same adversarial artefact generated against EfficientNet-B0 can produce almost no degradation in one black-box configuration and a considerably larger effect in another.

The clearest examples in this experiment are the Sigma Zero artefacts evaluated with Excire.

At distance 50, the observed accuracy reduction is **20.2 percentage points**.

At distance 80, the reduction reaches **25.8 percentage points**.

Again, these must be described precisely.

They are **transfer effects from artefacts generated using EfficientNet-B0**. They are not direct attacks optimised against the proprietary Excire system.

And these results are also not intended to establish a ranking between commercial products.

Instead, they demonstrate that robustness can depend strongly not only on the software, but also on the **specific configuration in which that software is used**.

The clean-condition results make this configuration effect particularly clear.

---

## Slide 9 — A lower miss rate can create a larger review burden

Excire provides a useful example because I evaluated three distance configurations: D20, D50 and D80.

If we move from D20 to D80, the clean-condition **missed-target rate falls from 12 percent to only 1.4 percent**.

From one perspective, this is clearly beneficial: fewer weapon images are missed.

But this improvement has a cost.

The **false-positive rate increases from 2.6 percent to 15.6 percent**.

At the same time, the proportion of OOD images assigned to the weapon-oriented state increases from **23.8 percent to 52.2 percent**.

So there is no configuration here that simultaneously minimises missed targets, additional review workload and OOD assignments.

This is why the thesis treats these results as **operating profiles**, rather than using a single metric to define which configuration is best.

The appropriate operating point depends on what kind of error an investigation is prepared to tolerate.

If the priority is to minimise silent misses, a more permissive configuration may be useful, but the analyst must accept a substantially larger review queue.

The separate OOD branch allows us to examine this third aspect directly.

---

## Slide 10 — OOD images were still routed to weapon

Every system evaluated in the experiment assigns at least some out-of-distribution material to the weapon-oriented state.

Among the proxies, the most striking result is the CLIP-based model, with an OOD weapon-assignment rate of **83.6 percent**.

In the six commercial configurations, the observed values range from **23.8 percent to 52.2 percent**.

There is a denominator detail worth clarifying.

For CLIP, each of the 500 OOD images is evaluated using five fold-specific checkpoints, so the 83.6 percent value refers to **2,500 responses**.

For each commercial configuration, there is instead one normalised decision per OOD image.

These values should **not** be interpreted as ordinary false-positive rates.

The OOD images are deliberately outside the supervised weapon versus non-weapon task.

What these values reveal is the behaviour of a **closed-set classifier when its basic task assumptions are violated**.

The system is still forced to produce an operational outcome, and this can influence the analyst's prioritisation workload.

This is another reason why clean binary accuracy alone cannot describe the operational behaviour of an AI-assisted forensic tool.

A selected explainability case provides one final illustration.

---

## Slide 11 — A maximally concentrated output can still be wrong

In this example, case 0015 has a reference class of **non-weapon**.

After the Sigma Zero perturbation, the EfficientNet proxy classifies it as **weapon**.

Even more interestingly, the maximum predicted-class probability is **1.000**.

So the model output is maximally concentrated on a prediction that is nevertheless wrong.

The Integrated Gradients visualisations allow me to inspect which regions of the image contribute to the selected proxy output.

However, I deliberately limit the interpretation of this analysis.

Integrated Gradients is used here as a **qualitative diagnostic tool**.

The attribution map does not prove that the model has understood the object correctly.

It does not transform a high probability into calibrated confidence.

It does not certify the correctness of the decision.

And it cannot explain the proprietary black-box systems, whose internal models are unavailable.

This example therefore reinforces an important general principle: a strong-looking numerical output from an AI system should not be interpreted automatically as trustworthy forensic certainty.

This brings us from experimental results to their operational implications.

---

## Slide 12 — AI should prioritize evidence, not exclude it

The results do not suggest that AI should be removed from digital forensic workflows.

On the contrary, AI can provide substantial benefits when investigators have to process extremely large volumes of material.

The question is **how that automation should be used**.

My proposed workflow begins with AI-generated tags, labels or semantic matches.

These outputs are then used to construct a **prioritised review queue**, reducing the initial workload.

But the next stage remains human review.

The analyst evaluates the context, verifies the material and is responsible for the final assessment.

Any action taken on the basis of the automated output should also be documented.

In particular, the process should preserve the **tool version, the configuration, the output-mapping rule and the validation context**.

This makes the workflow reviewable and allows another expert to understand how a particular item reached a particular operational state.

The practical rule emerging from this thesis can therefore be expressed very simply:

**use AI to prioritise evidence review, but never allow it to silently exclude evidence from human consideration.**

I will conclude with the three main points that I would like the commission to retain.

---

## Slide 13 — Conclusions

The first conclusion is that **clean performance is only a baseline**.

A system may perform extremely well under nominal conditions and still behave very differently when exposed to adversarial, anti-forensic or out-of-distribution inputs.

The second conclusion is that the different phenomena observed in this work must remain conceptually separated.

A direct attack against a source model is not the same as empirical transfer.

OOD behaviour is not an ordinary false positive.

And a configuration trade-off is not the same as adversarial vulnerability.

Treating all these effects as one generic measure of “accuracy” would hide information that is operationally important.

The third conclusion concerns methodology.

FAIRLab provides a way to connect the source image, the derived experimental condition, the model or software output, the normalised operational state and the final metric through a traceable and auditable process.

So, the central message of the thesis is this:

**Artificial Intelligence can be extremely valuable for prioritising evidence, but it must not become an autonomous mechanism for silently excluding it.**
